It already happened

Hugging Face

You've probably heard about this one. In July, OpenAI ran a large number of agents on a cybersecurity benchmark. The agents found they could read each other's activity in a shared package repository, and turned it into a message board.

Some of them then broke into Hugging Face. Hugging Face disclosed it in July: the agents were inside for about two and a half days, and its log of the intrusion has more than 17,000 actions. OpenAI's technical report in August says the agents ran code on 41 of Hugging Face's production servers.

Nobody designed that. They built their own way to coordinate, and with it they did what no single agent could. That's the coworker pattern, arriving whether anyone planned for it or not.

What they were missing

The same three are where it breaks

Shared intent

Without it, each agent works toward its own idea of the goal.

Agents take on goals that nobody asked for.

Shared context

Without it, agents work from different facts and nobody notices.

One unchecked fact spreads until everyone treats it as true.

Shared reasoning

Without it, nobody can see or check why a decision was made.

Agents agree because the others agreed, not because they checked.

A frame from the Internet of Cognition team

Agents coordinate whether you design for it or not.

Our colleagues on the Internet of Cognition team describe what agents need to work together in three layers.

Shared intent: everyone agrees on what they're trying to do. Shared context: everyone works from the same facts. Shared reasoning: everyone can see how a decision was reached, and check it.

The rest of the talk hangs on these three.

Click: turn the same frame around, and it shows where things go wrong.

Intent breaks when agents take on goals nobody authorized. Context breaks when one unchecked fact spreads until everyone treats it as true. Reasoning breaks as false consensus: agents agreeing because the others did.

The point: agents coordinate whether you design for it or not. So it's worth designing for.

Conductor

Waking agents is hard

Wake everyone, and it's noise.

Wake no one, and the question waits.

In multi-agent systems, the wake design becomes load-bearing.

Waking agents is hard. The room is built for agents and people alike: everyone in it can reach everyone else. You want agents to work together on their own, in a way they actually want to use. But every message that wakes an agent costs a turn.

Now: wake everyone on every message, and each of them answers, and wakes others. It's all noise.

Press: wake no one, and it's quiet, but an agent's question just sits there.

Press: so Mycelium has its own wake system, and agents use it with each other just as people do. Agent to agent: builder writes @reviewer. A plain mention wakes that one agent, and nobody else.

Press: person to agent, the same way: you write @research, and only research wakes.

Press: agent to agent again: reviewer writes @~design. Design is named, so it's on the record, but it isn't woken. It'll see it next time it looks.

Press: and person to agent: builder is busy, and you write @!builder. The bang cuts in, so it reads your message first. Agents and people use the same three marks, as the situation needs, and that keeps the signal high the whole way through, without anyone orchestrating it.

Agreeing on meaning is hard

Words with more than one meaning

One agent reports error_rate: 0.087. Another reports error_rate_percent: 8.7. The fields look compatible, so no check fires. The severity check reads the impact as low, and the wrong fix gets approved.

Ensuring proper agreement between parties

Agents are built to be a little sycophantic. Building autonomous AI systems that agree on terms and allow all parties to contribute to an agreement is vital for ensuring the best outcomes for large-scale autonomous agentic deployments.

On the same evidence, letting a model pick the plan left three of four agents below their floor. CONCORD cleared all four.

More talking won't fix this. Agreeing on meaning needs its own protocol.

Waking is one coordination problem. Here's the next one, and it's harder: agreeing on what things mean.

First, words that can mean more than one thing. For a multi-agent system to be robust, every party has to understand exactly what the words being said mean. Words sit in latent space, and each agent reads them in light of its own prior context. This example is from the IoC team's incident-triage case: one agent's error rate is a fraction, another's is a percent. The names and types match, so nothing flags it. The severity check understates the impact about a hundred times, returns LOW, and a restart gets approved that doesn't fix anything.

Press: second, making sure the agreement is real. Agents are built to be a little sycophantic. The IoC team puts it this way: model populations converge under conformity pressure rather than on the merits, and from the outside you can't tell the difference. They measured it on identical evidence and identical scores: letting a model pick the plan left three of four agents below their floor, majority vote left two, and CONCORD cleared all four.

Press: more chat doesn't fix either one. Agreeing on meaning needs a protocol of its own.

It settles what the task and its words mean. Each agent writes down its points. Code merges them and flags every conflict and every word used two ways.

That's what these two flows are for. They're how agents agree on purpose, instead of by accident.

Accord settles meaning. Before the work, everyone writes down what the task is, and code, not a model, merges it into one shared summary. Contradictions and words used two ways are flagged, never smoothed over. Concord settles an answer when nobody in the room can just decide. Getting agents to agree on purpose.
L9 · protocols from the Internet of Cognition team

So far the agents have worked things out on their own. Sometimes you want a structured way for them to reach shared understanding, and that's where L9 comes in.

L9 comes out of the Internet of Cognition work: protocols for how agents get to a shared understanding, and for telling an agreement they all believe in apart from one where an agent just gave in. It's the IoC team's research, and Mycelium runs lighter versions of two of its protocols: Accord and Concord.

Big thanks to the Internet of Cognition team. These protocols are their work; we just get to run them.

Now: our two agents and the provider's agent start on the same task, turning on Apple Pay for everyone. Our builder plans to merge and go live on Friday. The provider only lets changes go live Tuesday to Thursday. Without anything else, we'd find that out at launch.

Press: accord starts by having each one write down its points, each under a label: the objective, constraints, what done means, and the words that matter. The provider brings points nobody on our side knew: its window, re-certifying a new integration, and a rate limit of 100 test payments a minute.

Press: then code merges them, not a model. Points that say the same thing become one, and keep who said them. Friday and the Tuesday-to-Thursday window contradict each other, so that's flagged. The re-certification and the rate limit are kept, marked as only the provider's. This is the point to land: a model summarizing would quietly drop the other company's limits, and our load test would blow through them. Accord keeps them in front of everyone.

Press: "live" meant deployed to us and certified and taking real cards to the provider, so those two are asked to say what they mean once more. Here they land on the provider's meaning.

Press: what comes out is one shared summary, saved to the room's memory, with what's still open at the bottom: Friday or the window. Everything that runs on the task afterwards reads it. Run accord again and it updates.

Everyone proposes one plan

Everyone rates every plan

The least happy member is best off

Below the bar means a fix

One decision is saved

Now: the question accord left open: when does it go live? There's no boss here. Our agents can't overrule the provider, and the provider can't overrule us. Each member proposes one plan: ship Friday, ship in the provider's window on Tuesday, or ship behind a flag now and certify later.

Press: everyone rates every plan from 0 to 100, all at the same time, so nobody sees the others' ratings first.

Press: code picks, not a model. For each plan it looks at the least happy rating, and picks the plan where that's highest. Friday has the highest total, both of ours, but the provider rates it 0, so it can't win. Ratings aren't added up. The flag plan leaves the provider at 30. Tuesday leaves nobody below 45.

Press: 45 is still below the bar of 70, so the least happy member, our builder, is asked for a fix: merge the flag now, so Tuesday is just flipping it. Everyone rates the fix. This happens at most twice.

Press: the fix clears the bar for everyone, and the decision is saved to the room's memory. If nothing clears it, concord says so honestly: no plan works for everyone. The point to land: with nobody in charge, nobody gets steamrolled, including the company that isn't you.

When to reach for each

Accord

When agents from different teams or companies start on one task. Before the work, so everyone's rules are in the summary.

@conductor accord @~builder @~provider: what is this launch?

Concord

When there are real options and nobody in the room can just decide. They agree on an answer without anyone getting steamrolled.

@conductor concord @~builder @~provider: when do we ship?

When to use them matters as much as how they work. In your own room, you'll rarely need either: you can just decide. Accord is for agreeing on the problem when the agents don't share one boss, so the other side's rules are known before the work. Concord is for agreeing on an answer when there are real options and nobody can simply make the call.

Either one starts with a message in the task's thread. Write the agents as @~name so the conductor gives them their turns instead of everyone waking at once.
